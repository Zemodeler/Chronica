# Government: who may do what, per culture, around 270-264 BCE

Research for the government layer (see `docs/plans/constitutions.md` and
`docs/plans/departments.md`). Scope is 270-264 BCE, the opening of the Punic
Wars scenario in `packages/db/src/punic-wars-scenario.ts`. Later developments
are named only to be kept out. Confidence is marked **H**igh, **M**edium or
**L**ow. Where a claim rests on general scholarship rather than a page read
for this note, it is marked "(background)".

A caution that runs through everything: Polybius 6 describes Rome as it was
after 216 BCE, and Aristotle's Carthage is of the 330s. Neither is a snapshot of
270. The tables give the best reading for 270 and say where it is borrowed.

## Rome

Sources: Polybius 6, Lintott, Livy. The Republic in 270 was already the
"balanced" one: the lex Hortensia of 287 made plebiscites binding on all
(background), the plebs held half of the pontiffs' and augurs' places after the
lex Ogulnia (300), and the consulship was open to plebeians (367/342).

Key mechanisms:

- **Taxes and tribute.** Citizens paid the tributum only in emergencies, as a levy on the census, and it was repaid in part after wars (M, background). Revenue came from rents (vectigalia) of public land, mines, harbours and from plunder and indemnities. The censors let the collection of these revenues to companies of publicani in the five-yearly contracts (Pol. 6.17). The big provincial tithes belong to later conquests (Sicily from 241 and above all 210s on): keep them out of 270.
- **Coinage.** The Senate and magistrates with imperium issued; the mint worked at or near the temple of Juno Moneta; the tresviri monetales are probably later than their traditional 289 (source 6) (M-L). In 270 a consul or the Senate orders a striking: no separate mint office.
- **Spending.** The aerarium is the Senate's: no money without a decree, except what the consuls spend on the army (Pol. 6.12-13) (H). Quaestors keep the books.
- **Loans and debt.** No public debt in the modern sense: the state borrowed from citizens in need (the loans of 210 are later), and private debt was a tribune's grievance (nexum abolished 326) (background, M).
- **Grain and markets.** The aediles supervise the markets and the price of grain in the city; foreign grain buying and gifts of grain are the Senate's (M). A standing "annona" is later; keep the word out.
- **Customs.** Harbour dues (portoria) exist; they were let by the censors to publicani (M-L for the date and rate). Treaties may close or open a port (see Carthage).
- **Pacts, war, peace.** The Senate negotiates (embassies, 6.13) and the people ratify; war is voted by the people on the consuls' or Senate's motion; peace likewise (Pol. 6.14). Fetials make the declaration (background).
- **Levy.** The consuls hold the dilectus; allies send contingents per the formula (below).
- **Appointments.** Elected by the people; the dictator by a consul; the censors enroll the Senate; priests co-opt.
- **Justice and accountability.** No rendering of accounts at the close of an annual magistracy in 270; checks are tribunes' veto, prosecution (before the people, by tribunes or aediles) and the censors' nota. A standing extortion court (repetundae) is of 149; keep it out.
- **Terms.** One year; consuls iterate after a gap; prorogation is the Senate's, from 327 (source 7).

## Carthage

Aristotle (2.11) and Polybius 6.51 give the shape; the details of the 270s
are inferred (background; Warmington, Lancel, Huss).

|---|---|---|---|---|---|

(L) | Convene and chair the Council and the people; judge | Both preside; the council works through them | War, peace, finance: the elders | The Hundred and Four (M-L); the people when the elders split |

Key mechanisms:

- **Tribute and taxes.** Carthage took tribute in kind and money from the Libyan subjects, harsh enough to feed revolt in 241; it taxed trade and levied a property tax in need (background, Polybius 1.72). The Council decided rates; collectors were its officers. Flag the details L.
- **Coinage.** The state issued silver and gold in Sicily and Spain for army pay; the Council authorised, generals' staffs issued in the field (background, M-L).
- **Spending.** The elders control the treasury; a general draws on it by their grant. Aristotle's complaint that offices go to the wealthy implies recouping by corruption (H).
- **Customs and ports.** The state kept foreign traders to named ports: the treaties of 509 and 348 have Romans trade only in Carthage, Libya and Sardinia and western Sicily under the state's guarantee of payment, and bar Roman ships from sailing past the Fair Promontory (sources 9) (H on the treaties' text, M on the reading). The treaty of 279, made against Pyrrhus, is a defensive alliance (M). Philinus' treaty of 306 (barring Romans from Sicily) is denied by Polybius (L; see disputes).
- **Pacts, war, peace.** The Council decides; when it splits, the people vote (Ar. 2.11). Armies are mostly mercenary, hired by the state; citizens serve in a small sacred band and navy.
- **Appointments.** By election, by wealth and merit; one man may hold several offices (Aristotle disapproves; H).
- **Accountability.** The Hundred and Four judge men after office (H that a body of this kind existed, M on its exact power).

## Syracuse under Hiero II

| Charge | Holder and term | Alone | With a colleague | Needs the body | Accountable to |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| King (basileus) | Hiero, general by the army's and citizens' choice about 275, called king soon after (the date 270 or 265 is disputed, M); a co-ruler Gelon from 241 (later) | Taxes and the grain tithe, the royal mint, the fleet and army, the treaties, appointments, justice | None; kinsmen (Gelon, Hieronymus) are later | Formal assembly: kept in form (Hiero "ruled within the law", source 4) | His own interest and the city; no formal check |
| Friends (philoi) | A small circle of advisers around the king | Advise; the king decides | -- | -- | The king |
| Strategoi | Several; named by the king, one year for civic ones | Command garrisons and levies; town governors | A board | Orders from the king | The king |
| Tithe collectors (decumani) | Private bidders for each district | Collect a tenth of the harvest under the law of Hiero | Contract per district | The king's agents or the city's | Penalised for taking more than a tenth (source 5) |
| Assembly of Syracuse | Citizens | Voices approval, no initiative (M-L) | -- | -- | -- |

Key mechanisms:

- **Taxes.** A tenth of the grain, with the right to collect sold by auction each year, under a law that bound both the farmer and the collector (Cicero, source 5) (H for the law's existence as Rome later found it, M for 270 as most of the detail is Cicero's of 70 BCE).
- **Coinage.** Hiero's own royal issues, with his name and a diadem; mint under the king's control.
- **Treaties.** The king alone, e.g. with Rome in 263 (source 4); his friends give advice that does not bind.
- **Spending.** The king's chest: no distinction between king and state (M).
- **War and levy.** The king raises a citizen army and mercenaries; the Mamertine war was the occasion of his rise.
- **Justice.** The king and the old civic courts.
- **Succession.** Hereditary after him; Gelon associated 241 (H, later).

## Ptolemaic Egypt

| Charge | Holder and term | Alone | With a colleague | Needs the body | Accountable to |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|

| Dioiketes (chief finance officer) | King's appointee, no fixed term | Run revenues and the royal accounts; set the rolls | -- | The king for new taxes | The king |

Key mechanisms: taxes in kind and money, with monopolies (oil, papyrus, salt
and others) run by the crown; a royal bank; coinage reserved to the king
(a closed monetary system on the Ptolemaic standard) (source 11, M-H). Grain
is stored in royal granaries and exported by the crown. Customs are the king's
revenue, with duties collected at ports by contractors (M). War and treaties
are the king's, with his friends' counsel. No assembly of citizens binds him
(H). Alexandria's citizen body has its own bodies but no sovereignty (M).

peoples and cities, a land tax on royal land, and tolls; the king grants and

is hereditary with the risk of kin rivalry (H). Far less is certain about
fixed rules for provincial fiscal officers before Antiochus III (L-M).

Use (with gloss):

- Carthage: suffete (sufet, "judge"), the Council of the Elders, the Hundred and Four, the people (the assembly), the accounts, the general, the priests of Baal Hammon, the treasury of the temple (M).
- Greek and royal: basileus (king), philoi (the king's friends), strategos (general), dioiketes (royal finance officer), oikonomos (steward), satrap, nome, ekklesia (assembly), boule (council), synodos, damiourgos, euthyna (the audit at the close of office), telonai (tax farmers), decumani (collectors of the tenth), the royal chest, the tithe.

## Disputed and thin evidence

Most important first.

1. **Polybius 6 is later than 270 (M).** It describes a Rome after the Second Punic War. The Senate's control of the treasury and of foreign affairs is probably older (the Senate was long the guiding body) but the full "mixed constitution" picture is Polybius' (Walbank, Lintott). Simplification: use Polybius' competences for 270, but leave out the standing courts and provincial governors.
2. **Carthaginian bodies (L-M).** The "kings" are suffetes; the numbers of the Elders (some say 300, some 30 as an inner body), the Hundred and Four's origin (Aristotle names "the hundred" and Justin the 104), who elected generals, and whether the Hundred and Four judged all magistrates or only generals (see source 3). Simplification: the Council decides; the people only when the suffetes and Council split (Aristotle); the 104 try generals and magistrates after office.
3. **Syracusan kingship and assembly (M-L).** Hiero was a general before he was king; the date of the title (270 vs 265) and whether a civic assembly kept real power are unclear (sources 4). Simplification: Hiero is king, takes decisions alone with his friends' counsel, and keeps the assembly as a form.
4. **Philinus' treaty of 306 (L).** Polybius denies it; most scholars side with Polybius. Treat the 509, 348 and 279 treaties as real and the 306 clause as rumour (source 9).
5. **Number of quaestors in 270 (L-M).** Four before c. 267, eight after (and the scenario's four). Tresviri monetales date (source 6).
6. **Rome's iteration rule (M).** The lex Genucia's date and content are doubtful; ten-year interval is from Livy's age of repeat consulships. 
7. **Ptolemaic and Seleucid fiscal detail (M-L).** The Revenue Laws are of c. 259; they are used for a state of 270 on the assumption of continuity.
8. **Macedonian assembly (L).** How far the army assembly could hold a king to account.
9. **Allied obligations (M).** The formula togatorum is of the 220s; for 270, treat the amounts as unknown, the obligation as real.
10. **Roman debt and grain (L-M).** A treasury loan from citizens and Senate buying of grain before the annona are thin before 216.

## Proposed competence tables for the engine

Data shape: `act kind -> { mode, by, confidence }` where `mode` is one of
`alone`, `with_colleague`, `by_the_body`, `forbidden`. `by` names the office
or body. An office may also appear with `veto` (can stop another's act).
Acts: tax_rate, customs, coinage, loans, grain, pacts, embargoes, levy, war,
peace, appointment, spending.
